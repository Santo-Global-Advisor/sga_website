Rural land acquisition by foreigners is governed by a distinct framework: **Lei 5.709/1971** and **Decreto 74.965/1974**, administered by **INCRA**.

In **April 2026, the Supreme Federal Court (STF) confirmed the constitutionality of this regime**, including its extension to Brazilian companies with foreign-majority control. So these rules are not a legacy technicality waiting to be repealed — they remain fully in force, and the entity workaround that some buyers still assume exists does not.

## The rules that decide whether a purchase is possible

<div class="table-scroll">

| Key rule | Current standard |
| --- | --- |
| Who can buy without prior INCRA authorisation | A foreign individual who is a Brazilian resident, buying up to 3 "módulos de exploração indefinida" (MEI, a unit of area that varies by municipality), outside border zones, as a first such acquisition |
| Larger areas (over 20 MEI) | Require submission of an exploitation project |
| Municipal cap | Foreign-owned rural land in a given municipality cannot exceed 25% of its total area |
| Same-nationality cap | Buyers of one nationality cannot together hold more than 40% of that 25% limit |
| Border and national-security areas | Require prior assent from the National Defence Council |
| Non-compliant acquisitions | Void by operation of law (nulidade de pleno direito) — not merely voidable |

</div>

## The residency condition people miss first

Note the residency condition carefully: purchasing rural land generally requires the foreign buyer to **already hold permanent residence and a regularised CPF**. A visitor or temporary-visa holder is not, as a rule, in a position to buy rural land directly.

This inverts the plan a lot of buyers arrive with. They intend to buy land and then sort out immigration. In the rural case the order runs the other way: residence first, then the acquisition.

## Why the limits are in módulos, not hectares

The MEI is a unit of area that **varies by municipality**. The same 40 hectares can be comfortably inside the 3-MEI allowance in one municipality and well outside it in another.

This is why a figure someone quotes from their own purchase tells you almost nothing about yours, and why the parcel's municipality has to be established before any area calculation means anything.

## The caps that have nothing to do with you

Two limits can block an otherwise perfectly compliant purchase for reasons entirely outside your control:

- Foreign-owned rural land in a given municipality **cannot exceed 25% of its total area**.
- Buyers of one nationality **cannot together hold more than 40% of that 25%**.

If the municipality is already at its ceiling, or your nationality's share is, the purchase does not proceed regardless of your own eligibility. Checking this is part of diligence, not an afterthought.

<div class="callout warn">
<span class="label">Before you make this decision</span>

If a rural purchase is part of your plan, treat it as a distinct legal project from an urban home purchase. The area limits, registry procedure and INCRA oversight make this one of the areas where a wrong assumption is genuinely costly — and correction after signing is far harder than it is for a city apartment.
</div>

## "Void by operation of law" means what it says

Most legal defects in a property transaction are fixable, negotiable, or at worst voidable at someone's option. A non-compliant foreign rural acquisition is **void by operation of law** — *nulidade de pleno direito*.

That is a materially different exposure. It is not a defect you cure with an indemnity from the seller, and it is not something a well-drafted contract clause protects you from. It is the reason the diligence sequence for rural land runs entirely before signature.

## The diligence sequence that works

1. **Confirm your own status.** Permanent residence and a regularised CPF, before anything else.
2. **Establish the municipality** and the local size of the MEI.
3. **Check the municipal and nationality caps** for that municipality.
4. **Confirm the parcel is outside border and national-security zones**, or plan for National Defence Council assent.
5. **Calculate the area against the 3-MEI allowance**, and identify whether an exploitation project is required.
6. **Run ordinary title diligence** — registry, chain, liens, taxes, seller's authority. [See the property purchase guide →](../buying-property-in-brazil-as-a-foreigner/)
7. **Only then** structure the transaction and move funds.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming the urban rules apply.
- Planning to buy first and regularise residence afterwards.
- Converting hectares to módulos using a figure from another municipality.
- Assuming a Brazilian company places the purchase outside the regime.
- Ignoring the municipal and nationality caps.
- Signing on the assumption that a defect can be cured later.

## When this needs individualised counsel

Always. There is no version of a foreign rural acquisition in Brazil where the area limits, the municipality caps, the residency condition and the INCRA procedure can be safely assumed rather than verified — and the consequence of getting it wrong is not a penalty but a void transaction.
