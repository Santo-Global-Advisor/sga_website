Brazil is attractive to foreign buyers, but a property purchase is not simply finding a house you like and paying the seller.

Two things separate a straightforward Brazilian purchase from an expensive one: understanding that **urban and rural property follow genuinely different legal regimes**, and doing the document review before, rather than after, the money moves.

## Urban property

Foreigners may acquire urban real estate in Brazil without the restrictions that apply to rural land. That does not remove the need for proper diligence.

Before transferring significant funds:

- **Confirm ownership** and review the **matrícula** — the property's registry record — and the title chain behind it.
- **Check for liens**, outstanding taxes and condominium debts.
- **Confirm the seller's authority to sell.** Who is on the registry, who is signing, and whether they have the standing to do so.
- **Check municipal registration** and whether the built property matches what is registered.

<div class="callout warn">
<span class="label">Before you pay</span>

The cheapest part of a property transaction is the document review. The expensive part is discovering a problem after the money has already moved. A property being advertised online does not mean it is legally ready to be sold to you.
</div>

## Rural property is a different project

Rural land acquisition by foreigners is governed by a distinct framework — **Lei 5.709/1971** and **Decreto 74.965/1974**, administered by INCRA — with area limits, a residency condition, municipal caps and, in some zones, National Defence Council assent.

In April 2026 the Supreme Federal Court (STF) confirmed the constitutionality of that regime, including its extension to Brazilian companies with foreign-majority control. These rules are not a legacy technicality; they remain fully in force.

If a rural purchase is part of your plan, treat it as a distinct legal project. [See the rural land guide →](../buying-rural-land-in-brazil-foreigners/)

## Rent before you buy

For most newcomers, renting first is the safer move. It buys time to learn a neighbourhood before committing capital to it.

<div class="callout">
<span class="label">A question worth asking</span>

Is this the neighbourhood you would choose after living an ordinary month here, or only after a weekend visit? Renting first exists precisely to answer that question honestly, before a purchase makes it expensive to change your mind.
</div>

## Structure before you acquire

If you are buying as an investment rather than a home, the structure question comes before the property question:

- **Entity structure.** Holding, LLC and personal ownership carry different consequences for capital gains, rental income and repatriation.
- **Tax modelling.** Map the outcome on rental income and on eventual sale, not just the purchase price.
- **Funds trail.** Transfer in a way that produces clean, documented evidence of origin. This matters for ordinary purchases and is decisive for an RN 36 investor filing.
- **Execution.** Notarial registration, fund transfer and compliance coordination, sequenced deliberately.

Restructuring after purchase is meaningfully more expensive than structuring before it, and in the investor-residence case a wrong structure can invalidate the immigration position entirely. [See the investor visa guide →](../brazil-investor-visa-real-estate/)

## The purchase does not grant residence

Buying property does not, by itself, create a right to residence. There is a specific pathway — RN 36/2018 — with defined thresholds (R$1,000,000 in urban real estate in most regions, R$700,000 in the North and Northeast), a funds-origin requirement, and a minimum-stay condition.

An ordinary purchase, however large, that was not structured against those conditions does not produce immigration status retroactively.

## Common mistakes

- Buying before checking title, liens and debts.
- Treating rural land as if it followed the urban regime.
- Assuming purchase confers residence.
- Buying in the first months, before living an ordinary month in the neighbourhood.
- Financing into the qualifying minimum on an investor filing.
- Transferring funds without a documented origin trail.
- Relying on a seller's or agent's summary of the registry instead of reading it.

## When this needs individualised counsel

Effectively always, for a purchase of any size. Specifically when the property is rural, under construction, held through an entity, intended for rental income, tied to an investor residence filing, or being bought with funds from more than one country.
