Your relocation budget should be designed before you move, not reconstructed after your first confusing Brazilian bank statement.

## Opening an account

Requirements vary by institution, but a **CPF, identification, proof of address, and information about your financial profile** are commonly requested.

Non-residents and residents are frequently treated differently, and **not every bank serves non-residents at all**. This is the single most useful thing to know before you start: a rejection is often about the institution's policy on your status, not about you.

The practical sequencing problem is that proof of address usually comes from a lease, the lease usually requires a CPF, and a comfortable lease decision usually requires having been in the neighbourhood for a while. This is one reason renting first, and getting the CPF before you fly, tends to make the first month far smoother.

## Moving money to Brazil

<div class="callout warn">
<span class="label">Before you transfer</span>

If you are transferring substantial funds, keep documentation showing their origin — banks and, indirectly, tax authorities may request it. Receiving foreign money in Brazil is not automatically tax-neutral; the treatment depends on the nature of the funds and your residence status.
</div>

Two habits protect you here:

**Build a clean trail.** Transfer from an account in your own name, in traceable amounts, with the underlying documents (payslips, sale contract, portfolio statement, inheritance document) retained. Informal channels and third-party transfers create reconstruction problems years later.

**Know your residence status at the moment of transfer.** A transfer made before a tax-residence trigger and one made after can be treated differently. If a large sum is moving and you are near a threshold, the timing is a decision, not an accident. [See the tax residency guide →](../brazil-tax-residency-183-day-rule/)

## Build a real budget

Costs that foreigners consistently leave out of the first version of their budget:

- Housing, deposit, and the **guarantee** — Brazilian leases commonly require a deposit, a guarantor, or an insurance-backed guarantee, and the guarantee is a real cost.
- Condominium fees and utilities, which for apartments can be a substantial monthly line of their own.
- Transportation and health coverage.
- Documentation and professional fees — apostille, sworn translation, notary, counsel.
- School or childcare, if applicable.
- An emergency reserve.
- International transfer and exchange costs, which compound quietly across a year.

> Do not build your Brazilian budget using only your first month's spending. The first month is rarely a normal month.

## The exchange-rate question

If your income is foreign and your costs are in reais, your real cost of living moves with the exchange rate whether you think about it or not. A budget that works comfortably at one rate can become tight at another, and the timeframe over which that happens is measured in months, not years.

The practical response is not to forecast the rate. It is to build the budget with enough margin that a meaningful move does not force a decision you did not want to make — and to hold an emergency reserve you can actually reach.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming every bank has the same requirements for foreigners.
- Arriving without a CPF and losing weeks to the sequencing.
- Transferring large amounts without documentation of origin.
- Timing a large transfer without regard to residence status.
- Budgeting in your home currency rather than in reais.
- Treating the first month's spending as representative.
- Ignoring condominium fees and lease guarantee costs entirely.

## When this needs individualised counsel

- You are moving a large sum, or funds from a sale, inheritance or business.
- Your funds come from more than one country.
- You are near a tax-residence threshold.
- The transfer is tied to a property purchase or an investor residence filing.
