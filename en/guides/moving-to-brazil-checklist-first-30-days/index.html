Moving to Brazil: The Document Checklist and the First 30 Days

Relocation works better when you treat it as a project with stages, not as a single event that happens on moving day. Treat your first month as a transition period, not the final version of your Brazilian life.

One of the easiest ways to make a relocation harder than it needs to be is leaving essential documents until the last minute.

Build one digital folder before you fly

  • Passport, and prior passports if relevant to your history
  • Birth certificate
  • Marriage or divorce certificate, if applicable
  • Criminal-record certificate, when required
  • Proof of income or pension
  • Employment or service contracts
  • Bank statements or proof of funds
  • Property documents, if you already own assets
  • School records and vaccination documents for children

Depending on the pathway, documents issued abroad may need apostille or legalisation, and translation into Portuguese by a sworn translator in Brazil.

A simple system

Keep three versions of each key document: the original, a secure digital copy, and a working copy for applications. A simple numbered naming convention — 01_Passport, 02_Birth_Certificate, and so on — saves real time once multiple people and translations are involved.

Identify exactly which documents your specific procedure requires before translating everything reflexively. Sworn translation is not cheap, and translating a set you did not need is one of the most common avoidable costs in a relocation.

The six-stage roadmap

Relocation works better when you treat it as a project with stages, not as a single event that happens on moving day.

Stage 1 — Define the move. Why are you moving, for how long, with whom, from what income source, and toward what lifestyle?

Stage 2 — Immigration. Identify the correct legal basis, confirm nationality-specific requirements, organise civil documents, and plan apostille, legalisation and translation needs.

Stage 3 — Money and tax. Set a relocation budget, plan international transfers, review your tax-residence position in both countries, and build an emergency reserve.

Stage 4 — Life in Brazil. Choose city and neighbourhood, arrange initial housing, plan healthcare, organise banking, and start learning Portuguese.

Stage 5 — Arrival. Complete immigration formalities, regularise your CPF, set up practical banking, confirm long-term housing, and review your actual monthly expenses against your projected budget.

Stage 6 — Stabilisation. Review contracts and tax obligations, build local relationships, reassess your city and lifestyle choice honestly, and adjust the long-term plan as needed.

The first 30 days

Treat your first month as a transition period, not the final version of your Brazilian life.

Your priorities

  • Complete required immigration registration.
  • Regularise your CPF, if not already done. See the CPF guide →
  • Confirm accommodation and organise banking and payment methods. See the banking guide →
  • Understand local transportation and identify a hospital, clinic and pharmacy.
  • Start learning your neighbourhood before deciding you know it.
  • Review your actual spending against your projected budget.

What not to do

  • Do not sign a long contract you do not fully understand.
  • Do not buy property because you feel pressured to decide quickly.
  • Do not transfer large amounts without documentation.
  • Do not confuse a tourist stay with residence or work authorisation.

The best relocation decisions are, almost always, calm ones.

Family, if you are not moving alone

Relocating as a family is a different project than relocating alone — not harder by definition, but with more moving parts that need to be sequenced correctly.

Review every family member’s immigration basis individually before booking travel. The principal applicant’s approved status does not automatically cover a spouse, partner or child.

For children, consider school location, curriculum, language of instruction, transportation, and the adjustment period itself. Public and private education vary significantly between cities and even neighbourhoods.

And on language: even fluent English speakers find that basic Portuguese changes the texture of daily life meaningfully — not fluency on day one, but enough to handle a pharmacy, a landlord or a school meeting without relying entirely on someone else.

The final checklist

  • ☐ Passport valid and documents organised
  • ☐ Immigration pathway identified and confirmed against current rules
  • ☐ Visa or residence procedure checked with the correct consulate or authority
  • ☐ Every family member’s status reviewed individually
  • ☐ CPF obtained, or application planned
  • ☐ Tax-residence position reviewed in both countries
  • ☐ City and neighbourhood selected based on lived research, not photos
  • ☐ Temporary accommodation arranged
  • ☐ Long-term housing strategy defined
  • ☐ Healthcare arranged, including a transition-period option
  • ☐ Banking strategy defined
  • ☐ Emergency fund established
  • ☐ School arrangements reviewed, if applicable
  • ☐ Employment or business structure reviewed
  • ☐ Important contracts reviewed before signing
  • ☐ Property due diligence completed, if buying
  • ☐ Portuguese language plan started
  • ☐ Digital and physical document backups created
  • ☐ Professional support identified for the complex parts

Use this as a starting point. Your personal circumstances will likely require additional, specific steps.

When professional support makes sense

Many parts of a relocation genuinely can be handled on your own. Complexity changes the equation quickly. It becomes especially valuable when you have:

  • A family with different nationalities or immigration statuses.
  • Income or assets in more than one country.
  • A planned property purchase, urban or rural.
  • A business or investment structure in Brazil.
  • Significant international transfers requiring documentation.
  • Genuine uncertainty about your tax-residence position.
  • A contract you do not fully understand.
  • A complicated immigration history.
  • Several steps that need to happen in a coordinated sequence.
The point of counsel

The value of professional support is rarely about completing a form correctly. It is about seeing the whole picture before an irreversible step is taken — the wrong immigration basis, the wrong property contract, an unnecessary corporate structure, an overlooked tax obligation, money moved without documentation, or a city that does not actually fit the family.

Good advice should make the path clearer, not more complicated.
